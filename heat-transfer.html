CHAPTER 07

Heat transfer through the envelope.

How heat moves through walls, roofs and windows, and how a few layers of material decide how much energy a building needs and how warm its surfaces feel. The chapter stays with steady state: temperatures that do not change with time. Section 09 explains when that is not good enough.

All units are metric (SI). Symbols follow ISO 7345 and ISO 6946, as used in DIN 4108 and the Lehrbuch der Bauphysik; the US terms of ASHRAE and Incropera are listed beside them. Equations are numbered 7.1–7.13. Chapter 08 adds the flows up to a building heat balance.

01

ENERGY · TEMPERATURE · EQ. 7.1

Heat, temperature and specific heat

Heat is energy in transit: molecular motion in matter, or electromagnetic radiation in space. Temperature is its symptom. Heat flows by itself only from higher to lower temperature, as water flows downhill.

Energy Q · J, kWh
1 J = 1 W·s. 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 MJ. Heating fuel and electricity are billed in kWh (Btu in the US: 1 Btu = 1055 J = 0.293 Wh).
Power, heat flow rate Φ · W
Energy per time, 1 W = 1 J/s. A heater, a person (≈ 100 W) and a wall losing heat are all described by Φ.
Temperature θ · °C, T · K
Celsius: water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at sea level. Kelvin starts at absolute zero: T = θ + 273.15. A temperature difference is written in K: 40 °C − 10 °C = 30 K. Radiation laws need T in K.
Specific heat c · J/(kg·K)
The heat needed to warm 1 kg by 1 K. Metals 400–900, masonry and concrete 800–1000, timber 1300–2100, air 1000, water 4180 J/(kg·K), the highest of all common substances.
sensible heat to change the temperature of a massQ=m·c·ΔθJ(7.1)
Example0.5 kg of water at 20 °C in an 800 W kettle (efficiency 1.0): how long to boil?
Qm · c · Δθ = 0.5 × 4176 × (100 − 20)167,040 J
tQ / Φ = 167,040 J / 800 W209 s ≈ 3.5 min
  • Heating 1 m³ of air (1.2 kg) by 1 K takes 1.2 kJ; 1 m³ of water takes 4180 kJ, about 3500 times more. Air carries little heat; water and heavy materials store a lot. This returns in ventilation losses (Chapter 08) and thermal mass (section 09).
  • Latent heat, the energy of evaporation and condensation (about 2450 kJ per kg of water), belongs to psychrometrics and comfort; this chapter treats sensible heat only.

CONDUCTION · CONVECTION · RADIATION

Three modes of heat transfer

Heat crosses a wall in three ways. Conduction carries it through solids and still fluids. Convection carries it between a surface and moving air. Radiation exchanges it between surfaces that see each other, with no medium needed.

What controls each mode in a building (lecture frame 590)

STEADY STATE

In this chapter temperatures on both sides are held constant until nothing changes any more. Then the heat flowing into each layer equals the heat flowing out of it, and the same q passes every layer. Only the conductivity λ and thickness d matter; density and heat capacity drop out. This is the basis of U-values, design heat loads and seasonal energy estimates.

FOURIER · CONDUCTIVITY · RESISTANCE · EQ. 7.2–7.3

Conduction

In a solid, energy spreads from more to less agitated molecules (and, in metals, by free electrons). Through a flat layer in steady state the temperature falls linearly, and the flux is proportional to the temperature difference and inversely proportional to the thickness.

Fourier's law for a plane layer between surface temperatures θ1 > θ2q=λ·θ1−θ2dgeneral form q = −λ · dθ/dx W/m²(7.2)

The conductivity λ is the flux through a layer 1 m thick with 1 K across it, W/m² × m / K = W/(m·K). Its inverse, scaled by the thickness, is the layer's thermal resistance:

thermal resistance of a layer (Wärmedurchlasswiderstand)R=dλm²K/Wq=ΔθR,Φ=A·ΔθRresistances in series add: R = R1 + R2 + …(7.3)

  • Moisture raises λ, since water conducts about 25 times better than air. Design values (Bemessungswert, DIN 4108-4; ASHRAE design values) include an allowance for moisture and ageing; use them, not the lab values on a data sheet.
  • Direction. Timber conducts about twice as well along the grain as across it (pine 0.35 vs 0.14 W/(m·K)).

NEWTON · STEFAN–BOLTZMANN · ISO 6946 · EQ. 7.4–7.6

Convection and radiation at surfaces

The air next to a wall does not take the surface temperature at once: a thin boundary layer separates them. Convection and long-wave radiation act in parallel across it, and together they form the surface resistance.

convection (Newton's law of cooling)q=hc·(θs−θair)hc is not a material property: it depends on air speed, surface direction and roughness(7.4)

Every surface emits long-wave radiation in proportion to the fourth power of its absolute temperature. Between a surface and large surroundings the net exchange is

long-wave radiation, grey surface in large surroundingsq=ε·σ·(Ts4−Tsur4)≈hr·(θs−θsur)σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²K⁴), T in Khr=ε·4σTm3≈ 5.7 ε at 20 °C, ≈ 5.1 ε at 10 °C W/(m²K)(7.5)
  • Emissivity is not colour. In the long-wave range almost all building surfaces, white or black, have ε ≈ 0.9; bare metals have 0.02–0.3 (Incropera Table A.11; Bauphysik Tafel 1.8). Colour matters for sunlight: white acrylic paint absorbs αs = 0.26 of the sun but emits with ε = 0.90 (Incropera Table A.12). That is why a white roof stays cool.
  • Kirchhoff: at the same wavelength a surface absorbs as well as it emits (α = ε). A low-e coating reflects long-wave radiation back and emits little itself.
surface resistance: convection and radiation in parallelRs=1hc+hrinside: 1 / (2.5 + 0.9 × 5.7) = 0.13 · outside: 1 / (20 + 0.9 × 5.1) = 0.04 m²K/W(7.6)

U-VALUE · TEMPERATURE PROFILE · MOULD · EQ. 7.7–7.9

U-value and the temperature profile

From room air to outside air the heat passes the inside surface, each layer and the outside surface in series. Their resistances add. The U-value is the inverse of the total: the heat flow through 1 m² for 1 K between the air on both sides.

total resistance and thermal transmittanceRT=Rsi+∑j=1ndjλj+Rse,U=1RTm²K/W, W/(m²K)(7.7)
heat flow through an element of area A, and the energy over a time tΦ=U·A·(θi−θe),Q=Φ·tW, J(7.8)

Because the same q passes every layer, each layer takes a share of the temperature difference equal to its share of the resistance. Starting from the inside air:

temperature at the inside surface and at each interfaceθsi=θi−Rsi·q,θj=θj−1−Rj·qq = (θi − θe) / RT(7.9)

Why the inside surface temperature matters

  • Comfort. People exchange radiation with the room surfaces. A cold wall lowers the mean radiant temperature and causes radiant asymmetry: EN ISO 7730 and ASHRAE 55 limit a cold wall to 10 K below the opposite surfaces. Insulation keeps θsi close to the air temperature.
  • Mould and condensation. Room air at 20 °C and 50 % RH has a dew point of 9.3 °C. Mould grows when the surface stays above about 80 % RH, which happens below 12.6 °C. DIN 4108-2 therefore requires a temperature factor fRsi = (θsi − θe) / (θi − θe) ≥ 0.70 (with Rsi = 0.25), at every point including corners and junctions: at θe = −5 °C the surface stays above 12.5 °C.
  • Where to put the insulation. Outside insulation keeps the masonry warm and covers slab edges. Inside insulation leaves the wall cold, needs moisture control and leaves the junctions with floors and partitions as thermal bridges (section 07), but heats up quickly in rooms used occasionally.

CAVITIES · LOW-E FOIL · EQ. 7.10

Air layers

Still air is an excellent insulator (λ = 0.026 W/(m·K)), but an air layer is not 'still air'. Beyond about 15–20 mm a convection cell forms, and radiation crosses the gap regardless of its width. The resistance of a cavity therefore stops growing at about 0.18 m²K/W.

PARALLEL PATHS · ISO 6946 LIMITS · ψ, χ · EQ. 7.11

Parallel paths and thermal bridges

Real walls are not homogeneous layers. Studs cross the insulation, slabs and balconies run through the facade, corners have more outside than inside surface. Heat takes the easy path, and that path is also where the inside surface is coldest.

parallel paths with area fractions fj (Σ fj = 1): conductances add, not resistancesU=∑jfj·Ujupper limit of R; ISO 6946 averages it with the lower limit of Figure 4(7.11)
STUD WALL · EQ. 7.11 · ISO 6946 §6.7

PARALLEL PATHS WITH CURRENT VALUES

Between 12.5 mm gypsum board and 12 mm OSB. Real frames add plates, noggings and headers: a framing fraction of 15–25 % is common.

Thermal bridge · Wärmebrücke
A local area with a higher heat flux than its surroundings, and therefore a lower inside surface temperature. "Cold bridge" (Kältebrücke) is a misnomer: heat flows out, cold does not flow in.
Material bridge
A better conductor crosses the insulation: steel and concrete through the facade, a balcony slab, a lintel, fixings, window frames.
Geometric bridge
The outer surface is larger than the inner one: corners, especially three-dimensional corners at the roof edge. Heat lines converge and the corner is the coldest point of a room.
ψ · W/(m·K) and χ · W/K
The extra heat flow per metre of a linear bridge (slab edge, window reveal) and per point bridge (a column, a bracket), calculated numerically (ISO 10211) or taken from catalogues (DIN 4108 Beiblatt 2). They add to the transmission heat loss: HT = Σ U·A + Σ ψ·l + Σ χ (Chapter 08, Eq. 8.2).
  • A continuous concrete balcony slab can lower the corner temperature below the mould limit and add ψ ≈ 0.5–0.9 W/(m·K); a thermally broken connector (insulated, with stainless steel bars) brings ψ to about 0.1–0.2.
  • In well-insulated buildings bridges can add 10–30 % to the transmission loss. Draw the insulation line around the building section in one stroke without lifting the pen: every interruption is a bridge.

HEAT STORAGE · TIME LAG · EQ. 7.12–7.13

Steady state and transient heat transfer

Outdoor temperature and sunshine change every hour, and walls store and release heat. Steady state ignores storage. That is exact when nothing changes and a good approximation over long periods. When it fails, density and specific heat enter the problem beside conductivity.

Steady state · stationär
Temperatures do not change with time; q is the same in every layer; λ and d decide everything. Over periods longer than about two weeks heat stored and released by the walls cancels out, so steady-state U-values give the seasonal heating demand reliably (Lehrbuch der Bauphysik II 2).
Transient · instationär
Temperatures change; part of the heat entering a layer is stored in it. How fast a temperature change travels into a material is set by its diffusivity α = λ/(ρc); how much heat a surface absorbs from a change by its effusivity b = √(λρc).

STEADY STATE IS ENOUGH FOR

  • U-values, code compliance (GEG in Germany, IECC / ASHRAE 90.1 in the US) and comparing constructions.
  • The design heat load on a cold, overcast winter night (Chapter 08).
  • Seasonal and annual heating demand in heating-dominated climates (degree-day and monthly methods).
  • Inside surface temperatures and the mould check at a design condition.

TRANSIENT IS CRITICAL FOR

  • Summer overheating and peak cooling loads: sun and internal gains peak for hours; mass shifts and damps the peak (time lag, decrement; TAV in German texts).
  • Night ventilation and passive cooling, which only work with accessible mass (lecture frames 572–574).
  • Intermittent heating: churches, schools, holiday homes. Light inner surfaces (small b) warm up fast; with a night setback heavy and light buildings save about the same (Bauphysik II 3.2).
  • Passive solar storage (Trombe walls, slabs in the sun) and how much solar gain can be used at all (Chapter 08, section 07).
  • Touch and floors: a concrete floor feels cold, cork warm, at the same temperature. Skin (b ≈ 1000) at 28 °C on concrete (b ≈ 2200) at 15 °C meets at 19 °C; on cork (b ≈ 100) at 27 °C.
  • Only the first 10–15 cm of concrete or brick take part in the daily cycle (δ ≈ 110–140 mm): thicker mass adds little to daily storage. Mass must be exposed to the room: a suspended ceiling, carpet or interior insulation disconnects it.
  • Insulation has a high diffusivity but stores almost nothing (ρc ≈ 25–50 kJ/(m³K)): it is not thermal mass. Wood fibre, with c ≈ 2100 J/(kg·K) and ρ ≈ 160 kg/m³, stores more than ten times as much heat per volume as mineral wool at a similar λ.
  • Hourly building simulation (EnergyPlus, ClimateStudio, ISO 52016) solves the transient problem; Chapter 08 introduces the steady balance it builds on.

Model assumptions and precision

One-dimensional, steady conduction through plane, homogeneous layers with constant properties. Surface coefficients follow ISO 6946 Annex C with the inside at 20 °C and the outside at 10 °C; the radiation coefficient is linearised around that mean temperature. Air layers follow Annex D for large, unventilated cavities. The stud lab applies the ISO 6946 combined method; it does not replace a numerical calculation where R′T/R″T > 1.5. The dew point uses the Magnus formula (Sonntag constants); the mould limit is the surface temperature at which the room air reaches 80 % RH. Material values are typical design values; use product data and national tables for a real project. Section 09 uses a finite-difference solution for Figure 5 and the semi-infinite solution of Incropera Eq. 5.69 for the penetration depth.
