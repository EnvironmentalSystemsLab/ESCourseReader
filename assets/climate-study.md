# Site + climate: a design study

Develop three architectural responses supported by climate evidence.

1. Brief a client in one slide: read the monthly diurnal averages for your weather file and state, with the data, whether the climate has seasons and is sunny or cloudy, warm or cold, humid or dry. Propose first-order principles.
2. Draw a site section with true north, terrain, context, vegetation, solar access, wind exposure, drainage, and occupied spaces.
3. Document the weather station, coordinates, elevation, time zone, dataset, source period, and gaps. Explain the differences between the station and your site.
4. Select a seasonal temperature plot, a daily cycle, and at least one solar, wind, or moisture plot. Label units, filters, and time conventions. Write the design question each plot answers.
5. Propose three responses through form, zoning, rain, air, sun, ground, or materials. Draw a plan, a section, and an envelope or landscape detail. Explain the physical mechanism and its tradeoff.
6. Compare each response with an equal-program baseline using the same weather and schedules. Name the performance metric and a failure condition. Test one documented future or extreme-weather scenario.

Use this sentence structure:

“The data shows ____. Because ____, I propose ____. Compared with ____, I will measure ____. The strategy may fail when ____.”

Keep the evidence and conclusions distinct:

- Degree-days indicate outdoor temperature exposure, rather than building heating or cooling loads.
- Outdoor temperature bins are not indoor comfort hours.
- Horizontal solar energy is not facade irradiation or transmitted window gain.
- A wind rose does not calculate indoor airflow.
- Relative humidity alone does not establish the direction of moisture transfer.
- A typical year does not establish extreme-event or future-climate performance.
