IRRADIANCE · IRRADIATION · UNITS

Quantities and symbols

Solar geometry tells where the sun is. This chapter turns the sun position into energy on a surface: the input for passive solar gain, overheating checks, daylight and PV yield.

Irradiance G
Power arriving per unit area, W/m². An instantaneous value, like a speed.
Irradiation H
Energy per unit area over a period, Wh/m² or kWh/m² (per hour, day, month or year). Irradiance summed over time, like a distance.

01

LAMBERT · AIR MASS · EQ. 23–24

Cosine law

A beam of normal irradiance Gn that strikes a surface obliquely is spread over a larger area. The irradiance on the surface falls with the cosine of the angle of incidence.

A beam of cross-section 1 m² covers 1 / cos INC m² of the surface. For a horizontal surface INC = ZEN = 90° − ALT, so cos INC = sin ALT:

G=Gn·cos⁡INChorizontal surfaceGh=Gn·sin⁡ALT(23)

The low sun is weaker for a second reason: its path through the atmosphere is longer, so more of the beam is scattered and absorbed before it arrives. The path length relative to the vertical is the air mass:

m=1sin⁡ALTrelative air mass, ALT > 10°(24)
  • Above the atmosphere the beam carries about 1,361 W/m² (the solar constant), ±3.3 % with the earth–sun distance (Chapter 02, section 04).
  • At ALT = 90° (m = 1) a clear sky passes roughly 1,000 W/m² as DNI. At ALT = 30° (m = 2) typically 800–900 W/m²; at ALT = 10° (m ≈ 5.6) much less. Eq. 24 ignores the curvature of the earth and is not used below about 10°.
  • Both effects explain the seasons on the ground: in Ithaca the noon beam on a horizontal roof is 0.95 Gn on Jun 21 but 0.41 Gn on Dec 21, from a weaker Gn.
02

ANY TILT, ANY ORIENTATION · EQ. 25–26

Angle of incidence on a surface

A plane surface is described by two angles: its tilt TLT and the orientation ORI of its outward normal. INC is the angle between that normal and the sun ray.

In the site coordinates of Chapter 02 (east, north, up), the outward unit normal of the surface is

Ne=sin⁡TLT·sin⁡ORI,Nn=sin⁡TLT·cos⁡ORI,Nz=cos⁡TLT(25)

and the unit vector to the sun is S = (cos ALT · sin AZI, cos ALT · cos AZI, sin ALT), the vector of Eq. 7a. The cosine of the angle between two unit vectors is their dot product:

cos⁡INC=S·N=sin⁡ALT·cos⁡TLT+cos⁡ALT·sin⁡TLT·cos⁡(AZI−ORI)horizontal, TLT = 0°cos⁡INC=sin⁡ALTvertical, TLT = 90°cos⁡INC=cos⁡ALT·cos⁡HSAEq. 18, Chapter 04(26)
ANGLE OF INCIDENCE · EQ. 25–26

EQ. 25–26 WITH CURRENT VALUES

  • cos INC ≤ 0: the sun is behind the surface (or ALT ≤ 0). The surface receives no beam, only diffuse and reflected radiation.
  • On a vertical wall the beam is strongest when the sun is low and in front of it. A south wall at 42° N gets its highest noon irradiance in winter; an east or west wall in summer, early or late in the day.
  • A surface facing the equator with TLT = |LAT| has its normal parallel to the equatorial plane: at the equinoxes (DEC = 0°) the noon sun strikes it at INC = 0°.
CHECK

Eq. 26 contains Eq. 23 (TLT = 0°) and the facade relation of Chapter 04 (TLT = 90°, with HSA = AZI − ORI). Test a calculation by setting the tilt to 0° or 90° and comparing.

ISOTROPIC SKY · EQ. 27–30

Beam, sky and ground components

The irradiance on a tilted surface has three parts: the beam from the solar disc, diffuse radiation from the part of the sky the surface sees, and radiation reflected from the ground in front of it.

beamGb=DNI·cos⁡INC0 when cos INC ≤ 0 or ALT ≤ 0(27)

The simplest sky model treats the diffuse sky as equally bright in every direction (isotropic, Liu and Jordan). A surface tilted by TLT sees the share (1 + cos TLT)/2 of the sky dome and the share (1 − cos TLT)/2 of the ground:

sky diffuse, isotropicGd=DHI·1+cos⁡TLT2(28)
ground-reflectedGr=ρ·GHI·1−cos⁡TLT2GHI=DNI·sin⁡ALT+DHIEq. 23 for the beam part(29)
total on the surfaceGt=Gb+Gd+GrW/m²(30)

  • A vertical wall sees half the sky and half the ground: over snow its ground-reflected part can exceed its sky part.
  • A horizontal roof sees no ground; a soffit (TLT = 180°) sees only ground.
  • The isotropic model underestimates the diffuse radiation on surfaces facing the sun, because the sky is brighter around the sun and near the horizon. Simulation tools use anisotropic models (Hay–Davies, Perez) for the same three components.
04

WEATHER FILE · EQ. 31

From irradiance to irradiation

Annual and monthly energy on a surface is the sum of the hourly irradiance over the year. The hourly DNI and DHI come from a weather file; the sun position for each hour from Chapter 02.

irradiationH=∑hoursGt·ΔtWh/m², Δt = 1 h(31)
  1. Read DNI, DHI and GHI for each hour from the EPW (fields 15, 16 and 14, Chapter 01). Each value is the energy of the hour that ends at the stated time, in Wh/m².
  2. Find the sun at the middle of the hour: standard time − 30 min, converted to solar time with Eq. 2 (longitude correction and EOT).
  3. Calculate cos INC (Eq. 26) and Gt (Eq. 27–30) for the surface.
  4. Sum the hours of each month and of the year (Eq. 31). With Δt = 1 h the hourly irradiance in W/m² is the irradiation in Wh/m².
MONTHLY IRRADIATION · ITHACA TMYx · EQ. 31

ANNUAL TOTALS FOR THIS SURFACE

Bundled Ithaca Tompkins Regional Airport TMYx file (42.48° N, 76.47° W, UTC−5), the dataset of Chapter 01. Dashed line: horizontal surface.

Ithaca TMYx · irradiation by surface, kWh/m² (ρ = 0.2)
SurfaceTLT · ORIJanJunYearBeam share
  • A south wall receives a similar amount every month, while a roof receives three to five times more in summer than in winter: the facade suits winter solar gain, the roof is the larger summer heat load.
  • East and west walls peak in summer, when gains are least wanted. In Ithaca the west wall receives slightly more than the east wall, because afternoons are clearer than mornings in this file.
  • The best fixed tilt for annual yield is close to, but below, the latitude (about 30° at 42° N here): in this cloudy climate diffuse radiation favours flatter surfaces.
Model assumptions and precision

Hourly sums use the sun at mid-hour (Eq. 2–8) and the isotropic sky (Eq. 28). The weather file is a typical year, not a forecast. No obstructions, shading devices or self-shading are included: combine with the masks of Chapter 04 for a real site. The beam of hours in which the sun rises or sets is lost when the mid-hour sun is below the horizon; for this file the horizontal total is within 2 % of the file's own GHI sum.
