DESIGN QUESTIONS · SYMBOLS

Purpose and nomenclature

Solar geometry answers three design questions: which surfaces receive direct sun and when; where shadows fall; how far sun penetrates through an opening.

Angles use Szokolay's three-letter abbreviations. Colors are the same in text, equations and drawings: altitude, azimuth and horizontal angles, zenith, latitude and vertical shadow angle.

LATITUDE · LONGITUDE

Site coordinates

A site is located by LAT and LONG. Ithaca: 42.4° N, 76.5° W.

  • Latitude sets the geometry of the sun paths: noon altitude, day length, sunrise and sunset azimuths. All diagrams in this chapter depend on it.
  • Longitude does not change the sun paths. It only shifts clock time relative to solar time (section 03).
02

SKY HEMISPHERE · EQ. 10

Altitude and azimuth

Sun rays at a building are treated as parallel. The sun's position seen from the site is given by two angles on the sky hemisphere centered on the observer.

ALT · altitude
Vertical angle between the horizon and the sun ray. 0° at sunrise and sunset, 90° at the zenith. Negative values: sun below the horizon.
AZI · azimuth
Horizontal angle from true north, measured clockwise, to the vertical plane through the sun.
ZEN · zenith angle
Angle between the vertical and the sun ray.
ZEN=90°−ALT(10)

TIME ZONES · EQ. 1

Standard time and solar time

Apparent solar time
Time defined by the actual sun. Solar noon (12:00) is the instant the sun crosses the local meridian; HRA = 0. North of the Tropic of Cancer the sun is then due south (AZI = 180°); south of the Tropic of Capricorn due north; between the tropics it may be either.
Standard time
Clock time of a time zone. Zones are nominally 15° wide and use the time of a standard meridian (a multiple of 15°). Eastern Standard Time, UTC−5: 75° W.
Daylight saving time
Standard time + 1 h. Eastern Daylight Time: UTC−4.

The earth turns 360° in 24 h. One degree of longitude therefore corresponds to

24 h×60 min360°=4 min/°4 minutes of time per degree of longitude(1)

Ithaca is 1.5° west of the 75° W standard meridian. The sun crosses Ithaca's meridian 1.5 × 4 = 6 min after it crosses 75° W. All sun-path diagrams, sundials and the equations in section 07 use solar time.

SEASONS · TILT · DISTANCE

Earth's orbit and the seasons

  • The orbit is nearly circular: closest to the sun about Jan 3 (147.1 million km), farthest about Jul 4 (152.1 million km).
  • The rotation axis is tilted 23.45° from the normal to the orbital plane. Its direction is fixed in space.
  • The seasons are caused by the tilt, not by the distance. The northern hemisphere is closest to the sun in winter.
EARTH'S ORBIT

Three effects of the tilt

  1. Noon altitude changes by ±23.45° about its equinox value (section 06).
  2. Day length changes (Eq. 9). At 40° N, the Dec 21 day is about 6 h shorter than the Jun 21 day.
  3. Irradiance on the ground changes: the low winter sun spreads its beam over a larger area and its path through the atmosphere is longer. Chapter 05 turns this into the cosine law and the radiation on any surface.

ANALEMMA · EQ. 2–3

Equation of time and time conversion

The equation of time (EOT) is the difference between apparent solar time and mean solar time.

  • Two causes: the orbital speed varies with distance (eccentricity component, one cycle per year); the tilt of the axis relative to the orbital plane (obliquity component, two cycles per year).
  • Range: about −14.2 min (around Feb 11) to +16.4 min (around Nov 3).
EOT=229.2(0.000075+0.001868cos⁡B−0.032077sin⁡B−0.014615cos⁡2B−0.04089sin⁡2B)minB=(NDY−1)·360°365(3)
EQUATION OF TIME · EQ. 3

Clock time to solar time

Tsolar=Tstandard+4(LONGstandard−LONGsite)+EOTmin, longitudes in °WTstandard=Tclock−60 minduring daylight saving time(2)

INPUT

Example: Feb 25, 11:30 EST, Ithaca. Eq. 3 gives EOT = −13.5 min, so Tsolar = 11:30 − 6 − 13.5 = 11:10.5.

Analemma

If the sun is recorded at the same clock time every day, its positions form a figure-eight. The vertical extent is the change in declination (section 06); the horizontal extent is the equation of time.

ANALEMMA · STANDARD CLOCK TIME

READING THE FIGURE-EIGHT

Clock time depends on longitude: set the site and its time-zone meridian together, or pick a location. At Ithaca in February the sun is east of south at 12:00 EST (late); in November it is west of south (early). Every 1° the site lies west of its zone meridian shifts the whole figure 4 min later (Eq. 2). South of the equator the figure faces north.

MERIDIAN SECTION · EQ. 4–5

Declination and noon altitude

DEC is the angle between the earth–sun line and the equatorial plane. It equals the latitude at which the sun is in the zenith at noon (the subsolar point).

Reference dateDECSubsolar latitude
Mar 20/21 · March equinox0°Equator. Day = night everywhere.
Jun 21 · June solstice+23.45°Tropic of Cancer. Polar day north of 66.55° N.
Sep 22/23 · September equinox0°Equator.
Dec 21/22 · December solstice−23.45°Tropic of Capricorn. Polar night north of 66.55° N.

DECLINATION · EQ. 4

Noon altitude

At solar noon the sun, the site and the earth's axis lie in one plane: the meridian section. In this section the zenith angle at the site equals the angle at the earth's center between the site and the subsolar point, |LAT − DEC|. Hence

ALTnoon=90°−|LAT−DEC|(5)

For LAT > 23.45° N this is 90° − LAT + DEC. Between solstices the noon altitude changes by 2 × 23.45° = 46.9°.

Hour angle, altitude and azimuth

Three inputs give the sun position at any time: LAT (site), DEC (date, Eq. 4), HRA (solar time, Eq. 6).

HRA=15°·(Tsolar−12)T in h(6)

MODEL TESTING · EQ. 11

Sundials and physical models

A horizontal sundial with a vertical peg of length P records the shadow-tip position for each date and solar hour. Fixed to a model, it sets the model to any sun position under one parallel light source.

shadow lengthL=Ptan⁡ALTdirection=AZI+180°shadow tipxE=−P·SeSz,yN=−P·SnSz(11)
  1. Print the sundial for the site latitude at 100 %. Check the scale bar: the peg length must equal P.
  2. Glue it to the model base with its north arrow on model north. Fix a peg of length P at the marked point.
  3. Place the model in direct sun or under a distant lamp. The farther the lamp, the more parallel the rays.
  4. Tilt and rotate the model until the tip of the peg's shadow touches the required date line and hour line. The model's shadows now correspond to that date and solar time.

Model assumptions and precision

All interactive values use Eq. 3, 4, 6, 7 and 8 exactly as printed, with a 365-day calendar (2026). Cooper's declination formula and Spencer's equation of time are accurate to roughly ±0.5° and ±0.5 min; for design screening this is sufficient. Altitude is geometric: refraction (about 0.5° at the horizon) and the solar disk are ignored. Azimuth is undefined when the sun is exactly at the zenith.
